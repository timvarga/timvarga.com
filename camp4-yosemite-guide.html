03

A day on the Merced

Build in at least one slow afternoon by the water — it's the best antidote to a hot Valley July and to tired legs after a big climb.

Where to swim, wade & float

Sentinel Beach & Swinging Bridge — the classic sandy spots on the south side of the Valley loop, calm shallows with a straight-on view of Yosemite Falls. Easy to reach by bike or the free shuttle. Best for wading and lazy floating.

Cathedral Beach — quieter sand beach downstream with El Capitan looming overhead. Great late-afternoon light for photos.

Tenaya Creek (Mirror Lake) — cold, clear pools if you want to combine a short shaded hike with a dip.

Rafting: when flows allow in summer, you can float the gentle stretch from Stoneman Bridge toward Sentinel Beach; rentals operate out of Half Dome Village. By late July the current is usually mellow, but always scout for strainers and cold water, and keep well clear of any spot above a rapid or fall.

04

Natural history

Yosemite Valley is a granite gorge carved by ice and drained by the Merced. Knowing how it formed — and who lives here now — turns a good trip into a great one. Use the field guide below to name what you see on the trails in Section 02.

How the Valley was made

~100 million yrs ago · Cretaceous

Granite born underground

Molten rock pooled miles beneath an ancient volcanic range and cooled slowly into coarse-grained granite (technically granodiorite). These separate plutons — El Capitan Granite, Half Dome Granodiorite and others — are the rock you'll touch everywhere in the Valley.

~10 million yrs ago · Miocene onward

The Sierra rises & tilts

The whole Sierra Nevada block uplifted and tilted westward. Steeper gradients gave the ancestral Merced River cutting power, and it sawed a deep, V-shaped river canyon into the granite.

~2 million–15,000 yrs ago · Pleistocene

Glaciers widen the V into a U

Repeated ice ages sent glaciers grinding down the canyon. Ice quarries rock far more brutally than water, bulldozing the V into the broad, flat-floored, sheer-walled U-shaped valley you see today. Side streams left "hanging" — which is why Yosemite, Bridalveil and Ribbon fall from cliff lips rather than reaching the floor.

Throughout · ongoing

Domes peel, cliffs stand

Released from overlying pressure, granite sheds curved outer layers like an onion — exfoliation — rounding North Dome and sculpting Half Dome (whose "missing half" is a myth; glaciers removed only a modest slice along vertical joints). Vertical fracture planes gave El Capitan and the Cathedral Rocks their flat faces.

~15,000 yrs ago → now

A lake becomes a meadow

As the last glacier melted it left a rock dam (moraine) that ponded ancient Lake Yosemite. The lake filled with sediment, leaving the level, sediment-floored Valley — perfect for the meadows and the meandering Merced.

Three ecosystems on the Valley floor

~4,000 ft · sunny slopes

Lower montane forest

The dominant Valley woodland: open, park-like stands of big conifers over a sparse, aromatic understory. Fire-adapted — historically kept open by frequent low burns.

Look for ponderosa pine, incense cedar, California black oak, white fir; Steller's jays and acorn woodpeckers overhead; mule deer at the edges.

Along the Merced · shaded, wet

Riparian corridor

A ribbon of broadleaf trees hugging the river and side creeks — the greenest, coolest, most wildlife-rich strip in the Valley, and where you'll hang out in Section 03.

Look for black cottonwood, white alder, big-leaf maple, willows, Pacific dogwood; American dippers on the boulders; frogs, dragonflies and trout in the water.

Cook's & Sentinel meadows · open

Montane meadow

Seasonally wet grasslands ringed by forest, fed by a high water table. Peak wildflowers early summer; prime wildlife viewing at dawn and dusk.

Look for lupine, corn lily, monkeyflower, sedges; grazing deer and coyotes hunting voles; swallows and dragonflies working the air.

⚠ A NATURALIST'S TWO CAUTIONS: give wildlife room and never feed it — a food-habituated bear or deer is a dead one. And two locals aren't on the "charming" list: the northern Pacific rattlesnake (watch hands and feet on warm rocky ground; it wants nothing to do with you) and western black-legged ticks in brushy grass (a Lyme vector — check yourself after off-trail travel).
05

Two-night itinerary

Front-load the hard hike, protect an afternoon for the river, and keep mornings early — the Valley belongs to dawn hikers in July.

Day 1 · Arrive

Settle & stretch the legs

  • Set up camp; stash all scented items in the bear locker.
  • Watch climbers work the Columbia Boulder (Midnight Lightning) in camp.
  • Lower Yosemite Fall loop (1 mi) — right next door.
  • Golden hour at Sentinel Bridge / Cook's Meadow for Half Dome.
  • Campfire window is 5–10 PM; check the board for ranger talks.
Day 2 · Big day + water

Climb, then cool off

  • Dawn start on Upper Yosemite Fall (or turn at Columbia Rock), or shuttle to Happy Isles for the Mist Trail.
  • Back by early afternoon; refill water and snacks.
  • River time at Sentinel Beach — swim, float, nap in the shade.
  • Optional flat 12-mi Valley bike loop (rentals at Yosemite Valley Lodge, next to camp).
  • Showers at Half Dome Village; dinner at camp.
Day 3 · Pack out

Light, then leave

  • First light at Tunnel View or Valley View before crowds.
  • Easy Mirror Lake stroll and a last Tenaya Creek dip.
  • Coffee & the climbing exhibits in Yosemite Village.
  • Break camp by noon checkout; latch the empty locker.

Meal plan

Wheat-free Dairy-free Pescatarian

Cook fresh fish the first nights; shelf-stable tuna/salmon pouches carry the trail days. If anyone is celiac, swap in certified GF oats and tamari for soy sauce.

Day 1
Lunch — Salmon or tuna pouches with rice crackers, hummus, carrots, snap peas, fruit.
Dinner — Foil-packet lemon-garlic salmon with zucchini, peppers, red onion, over instant rice.
Day 2
Breakfast — Oatmeal with dried fruit, walnuts, chia, cinnamon, coconut-milk powder; coffee.
Trail lunch — Corn tortillas or rice cakes with sunflower-seed butter + banana; trail mix; salmon jerky; electrolyte tabs.
Dinner — Fish tacos: seared mahi-mahi or pouched salmon, corn tortillas, cabbage-lime slaw, avocado, salsa; black beans.
Day 3
Breakfast — Scrambled eggs with peppers, onion, spinach in corn tortillas; avocado; fruit.
Pack-out lunch — Cold rice-noodle salad (carrot, cucumber, edamame, sesame-tamari-lime) topped with a tuna pouch.

Snacks & cooler: trail mix, dried & fresh fruit, plantain chips, dark chocolate (check DF), roasted chickpeas, guacamole cups, sparkling water, DF marshmallows for the fire. Refill ice at the Village Store or Yosemite Valley Lodge.

09

Guide's safety notes

The WFR insists. In July the two things most likely to ruin a Yosemite trip are heat and the river — respect both.

Heat

Your #1 July hazard. Hike at dawn, rest in shade midday, drink 3–4 L on big days, and eat salty snacks — water alone risks hyponatremia. The Upper Falls switchbacks generate first-aid calls for dehydration every summer.

Water

The Merced kills people every year. Cold, deceptively strong current, polished granite near falls. Never wade above a fall or rapid; look downstream before entering; keep kids in arm's reach.

Bears

Locker latched, every time. No food or scented items in tents or vehicles, day or night. A clean camp protects you and the bears.

Reserve

Camp 4 is now reservation-only in summer. Sites release on recreation.gov one week out at 7 AM Pacific, booked per person (~$10/night). Six people share each site — book all three together and set an alarm.

Comms

Cell service in the Valley is spotty. Agree on meeting points and turnaround times before you split up, and download offline maps at camp.

Hike early · drink often · latch the locker · leave the granite better than you found it.
